Every Injectable Launch Needs a Touch-Up

A launch is a first pass, not a finished result

A
Aneesh Patel, PharmDFounder, Marga Partners
Launch strategyLifecycle managementInjectablesPost-launch feedbackCommercial discipline

Ask any great injector what happens after the first session and they will tell you the same thing. The first pass gets the patient most of the way there, and two weeks later they come back for a touch-up. Not because the first pass failed, but because a face is a living system that only reveals how it received the work once the product has settled, the swelling has resolved, and the patient has lived in the result for a while. The touch-up is where a very good result becomes the right one.

A product launch behaves the same way, and the industry mostly refuses to believe it. A launch gets treated as a finish line. The plan is set, the positioning is locked, the forecast is defended, as though the strategy could be perfected on paper before a single real injector touched the product or a single real patient lived with the outcome. It cannot be. Every injectable launch is a first pass, and every first pass needs a touch-up, because the market always returns feedback the plan could not have known. Managing a product well comes down to the willingness to go back and refine it once the real world has settled.

The launch is the first pass

The mistake begins with the metaphor. Companies picture a launch as a single decisive act, the moment a product arrives in the world fully formed, when it is really the first application of a plan built on assumptions. The trial told you how the molecule behaves under controlled conditions. It did not tell you how a thousand different injectors would actually hold the syringe, which patients would actually book, what they would compare the result to, or what they would say in the group chat afterward. Those answers only exist after launch, and they are the answers that decide whether the strategy was right.

A first pass done well should get close. Good pre-launch work narrows the uncertainty, and a disciplined plan lands most of the result in the right place. But close is not the same as right, and the gap between them does not close by defending the original plan more loudly. It closes the way an injector closes it, by looking honestly at how the result actually landed and adjusting.

“No injectable is finished at launch. The first pass gets it close. The touch-up makes it right, and the companies that skip it are shipping an unrefined result and calling it a strategy.”

What the real world tells you that the plan could not

The feedback that arrives after launch is not noise to manage until the plan reasserts itself. It is the most valuable information the product will ever generate, because it is the only information drawn from reality rather than assumption. Injectors show how the product behaves in hands that were never in the trial. Patients show which outcome they were actually buying, which is often not the one the label emphasized. The market shows what it compares the product to, at what price, in which position, and those comparisons are frequently not the ones the strategy anticipated.

This is where the two clocks a product runs on become visible. There is the clock of the plan, made of the launch narrative, the intended positioning, and the forecast. And there is the clock of reality, the slower and truer arc of how the medical community adopts the product and how patients experience it. The two rarely agree, and the launch plan was, by necessity, built to the first clock. The touch-up is how a company resets its strategy to the second one, the real one, once it can finally be read.

The refinement, not the reversal

A touch-up is a specific kind of adjustment, and its precision is the point. A great injector does not redo the whole face because one area settled unevenly. They add a fraction where it is needed and leave everything that worked alone. The restraint is the craft, changing exactly what the result asks you to change and nothing more. The strategic version is a company that reads its post-launch feedback and refines the one thing that is off, whether that is the positioning against a competitor it did not expect, the injector education the complication data is quietly demanding, or the price the market is signaling is wrong, without panicking and tearing up a plan that is mostly working.

That is the line between a touch-up and a reversal, and it is the line between a company that learns and one that lurches. Overreacting to an early spike, or to a single loud complaint, is the strategic version of an injector who redoes everything at the first sign of asymmetry, and it destroys the good work along with the flaw. The refinement is surgical. It assumes the first pass was largely right, isolates what reality has shown to be off, and corrects that alone.

The companies that never come back

The failure is not the launch that lands imperfectly, because they all do. The failure is the company that treats the launch as finished and never comes back. It ships the first pass, declares the strategy set, and spends the following year defending the plan against the reality contradicting it. A patient who is told that a settling, uneven result is simply the final result does not stay a patient for long. A market that is told the launch plan was correct while the evidence says otherwise does not stay convinced for long either. Refusing to touch up does not preserve the result. It abandons it.

The best practices in aesthetics build the touch-up into the promise. The patient knows they are coming back, and the result is understood as something perfected over two visits rather than gambled on one. The best product organizations should run the same way. The launch is visit one, the real-world feedback is the two-week review, and the strategy is refined into its right form once the result can actually be seen. A launch is where the work starts to settle. The touch-up is where it becomes correct.

The Marga Perspective

A launch plan is a hypothesis about a reality that does not exist yet. It gets built before a single real injector or real patient has touched the product, and no amount of pre-launch rigor changes the fact that the truest information about a product arrives only after it is in the world. Treating the launch as the finished result, and the plan as something to defend rather than refine, is the most common and most expensive strategic error I see, because it mistakes the first pass for the outcome.

The organizations that win treat a launch the way a great injector treats a first session. Get it close, watch how it settles, then come back and refine exactly what reality has shown to be off. A touch-up is not a sign the plan was wrong. It is the discipline that turns a good launch into the right one, and building that review cadence into how a product is managed, rather than declaring victory at go-live, is a meaningful part of what I do.
